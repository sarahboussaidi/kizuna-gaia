Water runs off the slope before it soaks in

Tanada — stepped terraces with overflow sills

Mountain villages flattened the slope into steps joined by low spill points, so water walks down the hill one level at a time instead of running down it.

Six documented connections

Where the two traditions actually touch.

Each is a real practice in its own country. What Gaia adds is the pairing — and an honest note about what is built and what is not.

Okuizumo  奥出雲

Reference

The Japanese approach

In Okuizumo, land left degraded by centuries of iron-sand mining was turned into terraced rice fields held by stone walls and fed by gravity canals from mountain streams. Village organisations keep the canals, and the timing of water allocation follows long-established custom meant to share it fairly and head off conflict.

The Tunisian counterpart

Jessour and meskat answer a drier version of the same problem: how to make a slope keep what falls on it. Tunisia has the earthworks; what is rarer is a written turn-taking rule for who waters when.

What both can learn

Japan shows that the social rule matters as much as the stonework. Tunisia shows the same engineering when water arrives in three storms instead of all season.

What Gaia would do with it

Treat the canal custom as data: a schedule with a fairness rule, publishable to a group of neighbouring farms. That is the Water Sharing module — designed, not built.

Source: FAO, Globally Important Agricultural Heritage Systems — Okuizumo.

Solar sharing

Reference

The Japanese approach

Panels raised high above a field, spaced so part of the light still reaches the crop, so one plot produces both food and electricity. The approach developed in Japan, where spare land is scarce.

The Tunisian counterpart

The oasis grows in three layers for the same reason: the top layer cuts the sun for the ones below. Tunisia has the sunshine Japan does not.

What both can learn

Japan brings the geometry — height, spacing, orientation. Tunisia brings the question of how much shade is a gift rather than a cost under a harsher sun.

What Gaia would do with it

A Solar Sharing Advisor that computes spacing for a given crop and latitude. It needs light-response data per crop we do not have, so it stays planned.

Satoyama  里山

Reference

The Japanese approach

The managed band between village and mountain, where woodland, fields and settlement are kept as one system: the forest supplies leaf litter and firewood, the lower slopes food and livestock.

The Tunisian counterpart

The Tunisian oasis is layered by the same logic, with palms, fruit trees and ground crops supporting each other instead of competing.

What both can learn

Both argue against the single-crop plot, and each gives the other a worked example in a completely different climate.

What Gaia would do with it

Model a parcel as layers rather than one crop, and tune the balance. Designed, not built.

Society 5.0

Reference

The Japanese approach

Japan’s national framing of technology as something that should serve a human society — including an ageing, rural one — rather than the other way round.

The Tunisian counterpart

Tunisian farms are not short of advice; they are short of advice in the right language, on the right device, at the right moment of the season.

What both can learn

The framing is what transfers: judge a tool by whether the person on the slope can actually use it.

What Gaia would do with it

Why Gaia plans spoken answers in Tunisian Arabic and offline use. Those are design commitments here, not shipped features.

NARO

Reference

The Japanese approach

Japan’s national agriculture and food research organisation, which publishes agricultural research and field results.

The Tunisian counterpart

Tunisia has its own agricultural research institutions, and a body of field knowledge that is largely unwritten.

What both can learn

A shared technique library only works if both sides contribute: one has publications, the other has practice nobody has written down.

What Gaia would do with it

The technique table behind the matcher stands in for that library. We wrote it by hand; it holds no institutional data.

Jessour & meskat

In the demo

The Japanese approach

The Japanese counterpart is the terrace with its overflow sill: level the ground, then control where the surplus leaves.

The Tunisian counterpart

Low dikes across a dry valley catch runoff and silt; a crop grows in what builds up behind them. Meskat gathers water from a prepared catchment upslope.

What both can learn

Together they give a hillside both storage and a controlled exit — the catchment holds, the sill decides where the overflow goes.

What Gaia would do with it

The one pairing the demo actually computes: enter a slope, a soil and a rainfall above, and the matcher returns it with three field actions.

None of these pairings has been measured in the field, and nothing here is presented as a result. The Okuizumo description follows the FAO heritage listing; the rest are described qualitatively on purpose.
